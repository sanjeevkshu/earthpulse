## What we publish

We cover all six content pillars — Our Planet, Through Time, Human Footprint, In Action, Voices & Research, and Take Action. Within those, we publish:

- **Explainers** — clear, well-sourced breakdowns of complex scientific topics
- **Research summaries** — accessible summaries of recent peer-reviewed papers
- **Initiative profiles** — case studies of conservation, policy, or community projects in action
- **Opinion and analysis** — evidence-based perspectives on environmental policy, clearly labelled
- **Practical guides** — actionable, researched guidance for individuals, educators, or organisations
- **Interviews** — conversations with scientists, practitioners, and policymakers on the front lines

## How to submit

<Callout icon="01" title="Pitch your idea">
  Open a GitHub issue titled "Article pitch: [your topic]" or email us at editorial@earthpulse.org. Include a one-paragraph summary, the pillar it fits, and the primary sources you plan to use. We respond within 5 working days.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="02" title="Write your draft">
  Once your pitch is accepted, write your article as a `.mdx` file following the content template below. Aim for 600–1,500 words. Include a Sources section at the end with links to all primary references.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="03" title="Submit a pull request">
  Fork the EarthPulse GitHub repository, add your `.mdx` file to the correct content folder, and open a pull request. Our editorial team will review for accuracy, tone, and style.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="04" title="Review and publish">
  We may suggest edits for clarity or accuracy. Once agreed, we merge the PR and your article goes live on the next Vercel deploy — usually within minutes.
</Callout>

## Editorial guidelines

### Lead with evidence

Every factual claim must be traceable to a primary source. Cite sources inline or in a Sources section at the end. Do not rely on secondary aggregator sites as your primary reference.

### Write for a curious non-specialist

Our readers are intelligent and engaged, but they may not have a science background. Avoid jargon without explanation, and use concrete examples and analogies to make abstract ideas tangible.

### Be honest about uncertainty

Science is not always settled. If a claim is contested, say so. If the data shows a range of estimates, give the range. Intellectual honesty builds reader trust more than false certainty.

### Distinguish science from policy

The science of climate change is consensus. What to do about it is a matter of ongoing debate. Keep these two domains clearly separated in your writing.

### Include something actionable

Wherever possible, give readers something concrete to do with what they have just learned — a question to ask their representative, an organisation to support, a behaviour to consider.

### Avoid doom and false hope equally

The environmental situation is serious. It is also not hopeless. We do not publish catastrophist content that leaves readers paralysed, nor green-washing that understates the challenge. Aim for accurate, motivating honesty.
