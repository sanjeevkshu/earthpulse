## Why the ocean matters

The ocean is not simply a large body of water. It is the engine of life on Earth. It absorbs roughly 30% of the carbon dioxide we emit each year and has soaked up more than 90% of the excess heat generated by human activity since industrialisation. Without the ocean's thermal buffering, land temperatures would already be catastrophically higher than they are today.

It also produces around 50% of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere — not forests, as is commonly believed — through the photosynthetic activity of microscopic phytoplankton drifting near the surface.

## Zones of life

The ocean is layered into distinct zones, each harbouring entirely different communities of life:

- **The sunlit zone (0–200m)**: Where most marine life is found. Phytoplankton form the base of the food web here.
- **The twilight zone (200–1,000m)**: Barely any light penetrates here. Animals often migrate up to the surface at night to feed.
- **The midnight zone (1,000–4,000m)**: Complete darkness. Life depends on "marine snow" — a constant drift of organic particles from above.
- **The abyss (4,000–6,000m)**: Cold, crushing pressure. Yet life persists — sea cucumbers and strange fish adapted to extreme conditions.
- **Hadal trenches (6,000m+)**: The deepest places on Earth. Amphipods and microbes thrive even here.

## Coral reefs: the rainforests of the sea

Although coral reefs occupy less than 1% of the ocean floor, they harbour approximately 25% of all marine species. They provide food security for over a billion people worldwide and protect coastlines from storm damage.

They are also among the most threatened ecosystems on Earth. Rising sea temperatures cause bleaching events. In 2016–2017, back-to-back bleaching events killed approximately 50% of the Great Barrier Reef's shallow-water corals.

## Ocean acidification

When the ocean absorbs CO₂, it forms carbonic acid. The ocean's average pH has dropped from 8.2 to 8.1 since industrialisation — a 26% increase in acidity — making it harder for shell-forming organisms to survive.

## What gives cause for hope

Marine protected areas have demonstrated striking recoveries in fish populations and biodiversity within just a few years of protection. The High Seas Treaty of 2023 set a framework to protect 30% of the world's oceans by 2030.

<Tip>
  Supporting Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) through advocacy or donation is one of the highest-impact ocean conservation actions available to individuals. Organisations like the Marine Conservation Institute track and advocate for MPA expansion globally.
</Tip>
