## The young Earth (4.5–4.0 billion years ago)

Earth formed from a cloud of gas and dust circling a young star. The early planet was a violent, molten world bombarded by asteroids. Gradually it cooled, water vapour condensed to form the first oceans, and the atmosphere — mostly nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and water vapour — contained no free oxygen at all.

## First life (3.8–2.5 billion years ago)

The oldest evidence of life dates to roughly 3.7–3.8 billion years ago. Around 2.7 billion years ago, cyanobacteria evolved photosynthesis — releasing oxygen as a byproduct. Over hundreds of millions of years, this transformed Earth's atmosphere in what is known as the Great Oxygenation Event — the most consequential biological event in Earth's history.

## The Cambrian explosion (541 million years ago)

Over a geologically rapid 20–25 million years, animal life exploded in diversity. Nearly every major animal body plan alive today first appeared during this period. The Cambrian seas were filled with bizarre creatures — five-eyed Opabinia, armoured Hallucigenia, and the fearsome Anomalocaris.

## Five mass extinctions

1. **Ordovician–Silurian (443 Mya)** — ~85% of marine species lost
2. **Late Devonian (375–360 Mya)** — ~75% of species lost
3. **Permian–Triassic (252 Mya)** — the "Great Dying"; ~96% of marine species lost
4. **Triassic–Jurassic (201 Mya)** — ~80% of species lost; cleared the way for dinosaurs
5. **Cretaceous–Palaeogene (66 Mya)** — asteroid impact; all non-avian dinosaurs lost; mammals inherited the Earth

## The rise of humans

*Homo sapiens* evolved roughly 300,000 years ago. If Earth's history were a single calendar year, we arrive at 11:36 PM on December 31st. The entire industrial era occupies the last 0.2 seconds before midnight — and in that fraction of a second, we have altered the planet's chemistry more profoundly than any event since the asteroid impact that ended the dinosaurs.
